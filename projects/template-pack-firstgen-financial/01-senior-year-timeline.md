# Module 1 — The Senior Year Money Timeline

**For parents who did not go to college and are figuring this out for the first time.**

Nobody hands you this. Counselors are stretched across hundreds of students,
and the colleges themselves have no reason to explain the parts that cost you
money. This is the calendar the process actually runs on.

---

## How to use this

Work one month at a time. Each month tells you three things:

1. **What's happening** — what the schools and the government are doing
2. **Your job** — the specific thing you do, not your kid
3. **Where people lose money** — the mistake that costs real dollars

You do not need to understand the whole system. You need to hit the dates.

> ⚠️ **Verify this year's exact dates.** FAFSA's opening date moved more than
> once in recent years. Every deadline below is the normal pattern — confirm
> the current cycle at **studentaid.gov** and on each college's financial aid
> page. When a date has moved, it has moved *later*, never earlier.

---

## The one-page version

| When | What has to happen |
| --- | --- |
| Spring, junior year | Run net price calculators. Learn your real number. |
| Oct 1 | FAFSA opens. File it. |
| Oct 1 | CSS Profile opens (only some private colleges) |
| Nov 1 | Early Decision / Early Action deadlines |
| Jan 1 – Feb 1 | Regular Decision deadlines |
| Feb – Mar | Verification requests arrive (if selected) |
| Mar – Apr | Admission decisions + aid offers |
| Apr | **Appeal window.** The part nobody tells you about. |
| May 1 | Decision Day. Deposit due. |

---

## Spring, junior year — the head start

**What's happening:** Nothing official. This is exactly why it's valuable.

**Your job:** Go to any college's website your kid mentions and find its **net
price calculator**. Every school that takes federal aid is required to have
one. Run it. It asks about your income and gives an estimate of what *your
family* would actually pay at that school.

Do this for four or five schools before your kid falls in love with one.

**Where people lose money:** Families look at the sticker price, panic, and
cross off schools they could have afforded. Expensive private colleges often
have the largest aid budgets — the $70,000 school can end up cheaper than the
$30,000 one. The sticker price tells you almost nothing.

---

## August – September

**What's happening:** Senior year starts. Application season opens.

**Your job:**
- Create your **FSA ID** at studentaid.gov. You and your student each need
  your own — do not share one. It takes a few days to verify, so do it now
  and not the night you file.
- Pull together: last year's tax return, W-2s, current bank balances,
  records of any investments that aren't your house or retirement.

**Where people lose money:** Waiting until October to make the FSA ID, hitting
a verification delay, and filing late. Some aid is first-come, first-served.

> **Jargon:** *FSA ID* — your login for all federal student aid. It's your
> legal signature. Treat it like a bank password.

---

## October — the most important month

**What's happening:** FAFSA opens (normally Oct 1 — verify). The CSS Profile
opens the same day.

**Your job:** **File the FAFSA. This month.** Not in January. Not "before the
deadline."

Some state grant programs and some institutional aid run until the money is
gone. Filing in October versus February can be a real difference in dollars at
the same school with the same income.

Then check whether any of your kid's schools require the **CSS Profile** —
roughly 200 mostly-private colleges do. It's separate, it's more invasive, and
it charges a fee (waivers are available). The FAFSA does not cover it.

**Where people lose money:** Assuming you make too much to qualify. File
anyway. The FAFSA is also the gate for unsubsidized loans and some merit aid
that has nothing to do with income. Filing costs you nothing.

> **Jargon:** *SAI (Student Aid Index)* — the number the FAFSA produces. It
> replaced the old "EFC." It is **not** what you will pay. It's an index
> schools use to build an offer. A low SAI is good.

---

## November

**What's happening:** Early Decision and Early Action deadlines, usually Nov 1.

**Your job:** Understand which one your kid is applying under, because one of
them is binding.

- **Early Action** — apply early, hear early, walk away freely. Safe.
- **Early Decision** — apply early, hear early, and if admitted **you are
  committed to attend.** You give up the ability to compare aid offers.

**Where people lose money:** Early Decision is the single most expensive move
a first-gen family can make by accident. You are agreeing to attend before you
know what it costs, with no other offers to compare against or negotiate with.

**If money matters at all in your decision, do not apply Early Decision.**
There is a financial-hardship release, but you are asking to be let out of a
promise, from a weak position.

---

## December – January

**What's happening:** Regular Decision deadlines, typically Jan 1 to Feb 1.

**Your job:** Confirm the FAFSA was received by every school. Log in and
verify — do not assume. Check each school's own financial aid deadline
separately; they are often earlier than the federal one and they are the ones
that control the money.

**Where people lose money:** Meeting the federal deadline while missing a
school's institutional deadline, and losing that school's own grant money.

---

## February – March

**What's happening:** Verification requests go out. Aid offers start arriving.

**Your job:** If you get a **verification** notice, respond immediately. It
means your FAFSA was selected for documentation review. It is routine, it is
often random, and it is not an accusation. But an unanswered verification
freezes all aid.

**Where people lose money:** Treating verification mail as junk. Aid does not
get released until it clears.

---

## March – April — read every offer carefully

**What's happening:** Decisions and aid offers land.

**Your job:** Take each offer apart. They are deliberately hard to compare.
For every school, write down one number:

> **Total cost of attendance − grants and scholarships = what you actually pay.**

**Grants and scholarships** are money. **Loans are not aid** — they are a bill
that arrives later with interest. **Work-study** is a job your kid has to
actually work.

Many offers lump all of it together under "Your Financial Aid Package" so a
$20,000 loan looks like a $20,000 gift. It is not.

**Where people lose money:** Choosing the school with the biggest "package"
instead of the smallest real cost.

---

## April — the appeal nobody tells you about

**What's happening:** You have offers. You have about a month.

**Your job:** **You can ask for more money.** Most first-gen families never do,
because nobody tells them it's allowed. It is allowed. It has a name —
*professional judgment* or a *special circumstances appeal* — and financial aid
offices process them every year as normal business.

You have a real case if anything below is true:

- Your income dropped since the tax year the FAFSA used
- A job loss, reduced hours, or a business downturn
- Large medical bills
- A death, divorce, or separation in the family
- Another comparable school offered your kid substantially more

**How to do it:** A short, factual, unemotional letter to the financial aid
office. State what changed, attach documentation, name a specific number you'd
need to make it work. No pleading. This is a normal administrative request.

**Where people lose money:** Right here — by never asking. This is the highest
dollar-per-minute action in the entire process.

---

## May 1 — Decision Day

**What's happening:** Deposits are due. The national deadline is May 1.

**Your job:** Deposit at one school. Decline the others in writing so waitlists
move for other families.

**Where people lose money:** Double-depositing to keep options open. It
violates the agreement and schools do find out — both offers can be pulled.

---

## What to remember when it gets loud

1. **Sticker price is not your price.** Almost nobody pays it.
2. **File the FAFSA in October,** every year your kid is enrolled. This repeats
   all four years — it is not a one-time thing.
3. **Loans are not aid.**
4. **You are allowed to ask for more money.**
